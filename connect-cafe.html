How a visit works

Three stops, one cup of coffee

Connect Café is built around the trial-and-error moments newcomers hit most often — and puts a person there instead of a help page.

01

Walk in, sit down

No appointment needed for the Social Area. Located near municipality buildings, so it's already part of a route you're taking anyway.

02

Ask a person, not a portal

Experience experts — fellow students who've done this before — share what actually worked for them. Municipality experts point you to the right forms and offices.

03

Leave with a next step

A transition checklist, an email to book a BankID slot, or just the name of the person to ask next. Small, concrete, yours to keep.

Project Process Journal · Design Thinking course

The story behind Connect Café

How a team of four exchange students turned their own confusion navigating Swedish digital life into a physical, human-first concept — using the Double Diamond process.

Diverge, then converge — twice. Research and reframing on the left; ideation and testing on the right.

Discover◦ 01 ◦
Understanding the users

The team mapped the ecosystem around a newcomer — government, banks, BankID, social norms, tech companies — and found the user sitting at the edge of it, not the center. Twelve needfinding interviews with international students followed, sorted into an affinity diagram of four clusters: Inclusion, Dependency, Guidance and Isolation.

Pattern: Human contact

The lack of human aid in the transition phase leads to long, lonely stretches of trial and error.

Pattern: Sense of belonging

Social distance in Swedish society leaves newcomers feeling like outsiders even once the paperwork is done.

Pattern: Willingness

Newcomers want to integrate — the effort is there, but the entry points aren't easy to find.

Pattern: Infrastructure

Participating in Swedish life runs through its digital infrastructure — with no easy on-ramp for someone new to it.

Define◦ 02 ◦
Reframing the problem

Inspired by three artworks around SSE's campus — an ocean, two friends talking, a pile of clothing — the team reframed the challenge: not a digital system too complex to fix, but a transition phase with no human support built in.

Who

New-coming international students, tech-savvy but unfamiliar with Sweden's digital system.

Need

A way to receive responsive, in-person support that reduces the complexity of the transition phase.

Why

Because the current process only offers detached information — leaving newcomers feeling estranged.

How might we…

involve real people in the transition? lower the threshold to enter the system? make in-person guidance scalable? break language barriers? use existing resources?
Develop◦ 03 ◦
Generating concepts

Several rounds of brainstorming — including one round with ChatGPT as a fourth participant — produced a wide field of ideas, later clustered into three directions.

Buddy system

Newcomers matched to a personal buddy for detailed, ongoing guidance.

  • + Personal contact
  • − Time-intensive, expensive

AI Helpdesk

An always-on chatbot giving fast, tailored answers.

  • + Quick, detailed
  • − No human contact
Chosen

Tandem Transition Café

A physical place to ask questions over coffee — direct, personal, close to municipal offices.

  • + Physical, personalised help
  • − Real-estate cost
Deliver◦ 04 ◦
Prototyping & testing

The idea was renamed Connect Café for clarity, then built as a lo-fi physical prototype — clay, paper and pom-poms standing in for a Stockholm café — and a parallel digital walkthrough. Eight users tested both, acting out real scenarios like arranging a BankID.

Lo-fi physical model

A scaled Stockholm map leading into a hand-built café interior with a Social Area, Expert Corner and Question Box.

Digital walkthrough

A simple virtual café — enter, get a Fika, ask a question — used to sanity-check the physical prototype's logic.

User testing

Think-aloud sessions with 8 international students, following a "show, don't tell" approach.

What landed

  • The checklist was the standout feature — "a place where I discovered things I didn't even know existed."
  • The café's social, low-threshold framing felt genuinely welcoming.

What surprised the team

  • Some testers didn't realise it was a café simulation at all.
  • An AI-style chat element read as untrustworthy to some.
  • Anonymity wasn't universally wanted.

Assumptions it debunked

  • Users wanted only what was relevant to them — not all available information.
  • The café metaphor needed explicit framing, not assumed intuition.
  • A social setting can feel overwhelming, not just inviting, for introverted users.
Reflection

What the team took away

David

Shared personal experience with the challenge made it easier to empathise with users and align quickly on what felt meaningful.

Ruiling

Reframing through art, and thinking with sticky notes, made abstract problems intuitive to organise and make sense of as a team.

Amira

Design thinking proved most useful in fields where desirability isn't the only focus — it's a tool for uncovering needs that are otherwise hard to see.
